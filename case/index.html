How this site was built

Fernrow Bakehouse is invented. The website is real — hand-written, running, and measured. This page walks through what is on it, where the Order and Book buttons lead, and what the whole thing weighs.

What it is
One page, plus two short handoff screens. No blog, no login, no second menu.
Built with
Hand-written HTML and CSS, 3 KB of JavaScript. No framework, no build step.
First mobile visit
165 KB across nine requests, photography included.
Measured
Lighthouse 97 on mobile, 100 on desktop. Method further down.

The brief, written by me

No client, so the requirements had to come from somewhere honest: the questions people actually open a café's website to answer.

Someone standing on the pavement with a phone in one hand wants four things, roughly in this order: Is it open right now? What is on, and what does it cost? Where is it? How do I order, or hold a table?

Every section of the page answers one of those. Anything that answered none of them did not get built.

Why one page. A café's website gets opened on a phone, usually by someone already close by, usually for less than a minute. Splitting the menu, the hours and the address across four pages adds three taps and three chances to give up. So: one page, in the order the questions get asked.

Where the buttons actually go

This is the part worth being plain about. The site does not take orders and does not hold tables. It hands the visitor to the systems the café already pays for, in one tap, with the decision already made.

Ordering · via Toast

Order online

Every Order button on the site points at one destination. In this concept that is a handoff screen standing in for a Toast online-ordering page, so the whole journey can be walked without sending anyone to a real café's checkout.

On a real build the button carries the café's own ordering URL — Toast, Square, Deliveroo, Uber Eats, ChowNow, whatever they already run.

Reservations · via Resy

Book a table

Same shape. Book a table opens a matching handoff screen in place of a Resy booking page, and the reservation itself would be made in Resy.

A café that takes bookings by phone instead gets a tap-to-call button in the same position, and the diagram loses its middle step.

Nothing here processes a payment. No cart, no card field, no account, no database — the site is a folder of static files. That is deliberate. The ordering platform a café already uses is wired to their kitchen printer, their stock counts and their payouts, and replacing it is not what this build is for. Feeding it cleanly is.

Nine requests is the entire first visit on a phone: the page itself, one stylesheet, two subset fonts, one script, one photograph, and the small remainder — the web manifest and the icon. Desktop weighs more, not less, because a wide screen is sent a wider hero and reaches a second full-width photograph before the measurement ends.

The mobile Speed Index is the one figure that moves between runs, and it is the reason the mobile score is 97 rather than 100. Five of the six runs measured about 3.9 s; one measured 1.6 s. Everything else stayed put — same paint times, same layout shift, same bytes, same requests. The cause is the display font: on a deliberately throttled connection it sometimes arrives before the first paint and sometimes just after, and Speed Index scores how finished the screen looks rather than how soon it is usable. The alternative is to let the page paint in a fallback face and never swap, which reads worse and measured worse for accessibility, so the font stays. I am quoting the slower of the two numbers.

Two flags stay open on purpose. Lighthouse would like the stylesheet inlined into the page rather than fetched, and it would like the CSS minified. It estimates the first of those at up to 150 ms on a throttled phone and the second at about 2 KiB. Both need a build step, and this project does not have one — the files that get served are the files that were written, comments and all, which is what makes them straightforward to hand over and edit a year later. That was a deliberate trade, and this is the size of it.

What's on the page, and what isn't

A one-page site is defined as much by what it leaves out. Both columns are decisions, not gaps.

On the page

  • One page built around the café's own menu, hours, address and photographs
  • The full menu as selectable text — sections, prices, dietary marks
  • Hours that highlight today and name the next opening
  • Order and Book buttons wired to the platforms the café already uses
  • Address, a locator map, and a directions link that opens the visitor's map app
  • Tap-to-call number, email, and a short message form
  • Photographs cropped, graded and encoded at a width for each screen size
  • Hours, address and coordinates in machine-readable markup
  • Title, description, social preview card, icon, web manifest, 404 page
  • Two handoff screens, and hosting with the site running on it

Deliberately not here

  • Ordering, carts and card payments — those stay on the café's platform
  • A booking engine, table map or waitlist — bookings stay in their tool
  • Till, POS or stock integration
  • Delivery zones, postcode checks, dispatch
  • A blog, a login, customer accounts
  • A second page for anything the one page already answers
  • A photographer on location — the café's own photographs are prepared and used
  • Multi-location directories, franchise pages, a webshop
  • Cookie banners, chat bubbles, popups, newsletter interruptions
  • Analytics or tracking scripts of any kind on this build

One honest catch. The ordering platform already holds a menu, and now the website holds one too — two places to edit when the soup changes. Worth knowing before you commission one. It is still worth having: the menu inside an ordering platform sits behind a tap, is invisible to search engines, and prices are the second thing anybody looks for. So on this build the page carries the readable menu, and the ordering platform stays the record of what is actually available today.

Three things this page is evidence for

Only three, and each one is visible in the build above rather than asserted.

01

Nothing to buy, nothing to set up

No plugin licence, no theme purchase, no monthly builder subscription, no dashboard to learn. The whole site is a folder of files on hosting that comes with it. There is nothing here a café owner has to configure, and nothing that quietly starts charging in month two.

02

A short timeline, because the scope is short

Three days is realistic for this because of the right-hand column above. One page, no ordering system to integrate, no booking engine to configure — the work is design, copy, photography and wiring, and all of it fits in days rather than weeks.

03

Your menu, your photographs, your links

The menu above is set as a real menu, in the sections a bakehouse actually uses, with prices that make sense together. That is the difference between a site built around a café and a restaurant template with the words swapped out.
